# Results (2026-10-20)

Findings from the completed study, written to support the report. Every number below comes
from `results/summary/tables.md` (generated by `experiments/summarize.py` from the saved runs);
figures are in `plots/` (generated by `experiments/make_plots.py`). Design decisions referred to
as Dn are in `docs/DECISIONS.md`; how each step was built and tested is in `docs/PROGRESS.md`.

## Setup in one paragraph

MNIST, a small CNN (d = 46,730 parameters), 20 honest clients with a Dirichlet(α = 0.5) label
split, 20 rounds of FedAvg with one local epoch. The attacker runs **8 Sybils** (28 clients in
total, 29%) that each train on 500 of 1,000 withheld sevens relabelled as 1 (D9), so the attack
target is 7 → 1 and its success rate (ASR) is the share of test sevens predicted as 1. The
defence projects each client's normalised update with a fresh secret ±1/√k matrix per round —
round key from HKDF-SHA256, matrix bits from keyed SHAKE-256 (D3) — then clusters by cosine
similarity (average linkage, threshold τ) and drops clusters of ≥ 3 clients before FedAvg
(D11). The full-dimensional counterpart is the same detector without projection. Thresholds
were tuned on seed 0 only (full: τ = 0.25; projected k = 64: τ = 0.15) and results use held-out
seeds. Accuracy and ASR are means over rounds 11–20 (ASR oscillates between rounds); detection
scores are summed over all 20 rounds.

## Headline: what the secret key buys (E6, Figure 5)

After local training, each Sybil adds a perturbation of size ε·‖update‖ to what it submits,
trying to stop looking like the other Sybils (D10).

| Attacker | Smallest budget that evades the projected detector | Recall there | ASR there | Full-dim detector recall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Knows R_t (key leaked) | **ε = 0.02** | 0.000 | 0.189 ± 0.044 | 1.000 |
| Guesses R_t (key secret) | ε = 0.5 (ε ≤ 0.2 fully detected) | 0.084 ± 0.093 | 0.154 ± 0.087 | 0.997 |
| Gaussian noise | ε = 0.5 (ε ≤ 0.2 fully detected) | 0.122 ± 0.013 | 0.146 ± 0.009 | 1.000 |

(Seeds 1–2; budgets tested 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5. Undefended FedAvg ASR: 0.182.)

- **With the key leaked, a 2% change defeats the projected detector completely** and the attack
  recovers its full undefended strength (ASR 0.18–0.19 at every ε ≥ 0.02), while the same
  perturbed updates are still caught by the full-dimensional detector (recall 1.000). The
  perturbation lives in the matrix's 64-dimensional row space, which the projection magnifies
  by about √(d/k) ≈ 27.
- **With the key secret, the attacker cannot aim**: its own guessed matrix is unrelated to R_t,
  so its perturbation behaves exactly like random noise (the guessed and Gaussian rows match at
  every budget). Evasion then needs ε between 0.2 and 0.5, against 0.01–0.02 with the key: **at
  least 10× and up to 50× more perturbation** (the grid is coarse), consistent with the
  √(d/k) ≈ 27 prediction — and even at ε = 0.5 it is only partial (recall 0.08–0.12) and the
  attack is weaker (ASR 0.15 vs 0.19).
- **The projection has a security cost that the key pays for.** Projection opens a cheap evasion
  route (row-space perturbations) that the full-dimensional detector does not have. Keeping R_t
  secret and fresh each round closes it. This is the cryptographic contribution: under the PRF
  assumptions of D3 the attacker cannot predict R_t, and in this experiment that is what
  separates "evaded at 2%" from "detected up to 20%".
- **Nuance at ε = 0.5:** even the key-secret attacker evades the projected detector while the
  full-dimensional one still detects it. That is a threshold effect, not the key's: the
  projected detector needs a tighter τ (0.15 vs 0.25) to keep false positives down despite
  projection distortion (E1), which leaves it less margin against large isotropic noise.

## E2 — the defences under attack (Figures 1–2)

Held-out seeds 1–3, mean ± std.

| Scenario | Defence | Accuracy | ASR | Detection F1 | FPR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| no attack | FedAvg | 0.9828 ± 0.0009 | 0.0041 | — | — |
| no attack | coordinate median | 0.9795 ± 0.0007 | 0.0049 | — | — |
| no attack | full-dim detector | 0.9827 ± 0.0008 | 0.0040 | — | 0.011 ± 0.004 |
| no attack | projected detector | 0.9828 ± 0.0009 | 0.0041 | — | 0.000 |
| attack | FedAvg | 0.9617 ± 0.0032 | **0.1815 ± 0.0374** | — | — |
| attack | coordinate median | 0.9532 ± 0.0014 | 0.0750 ± 0.0144 | — | — |
| attack | full-dim detector | 0.9828 ± 0.0008 | 0.0039 ± 0.0009 | 0.989 ± 0.002 | 0.009 ± 0.001 |
| attack | projected detector | 0.9828 ± 0.0008 | **0.0038 ± 0.0008** | **1.000** | **0.000** |

- Undefended, the 8 Sybils raise ASR from 0.004 to 0.18 (oscillating strongly between rounds,
  Figure 2) and cost ~2 points of accuracy.
- Both detectors remove the attack completely: ASR and accuracy equal the no-attack run. No round
  was ever skipped. The projected detector caught every Sybil in every round with no false
  positives; the full-dimensional detector flagged a few honest clients (FPR ≈ 1%), because its
  tuned τ sits at the edge of its seed-0 plateau — not because projection detects better.
- The coordinate median, which needs no detector, only halves the attack (ASR 0.075) and costs
  accuracy with or without an attack (0.953 / 0.980).
- With no attack the detectors cost nothing in accuracy.
- Caveat: these Sybils are near-duplicates (pairwise cosine ≈ 0.98, honest pairs ≈ 0.16), so
  unevaded detection is easy for any similarity detector; E6 is the hard case.

## E1 — does projection preserve similarity? (Figure 4, Figure 3)

Absolute error of projected vs full cosine similarity over all client pairs, real client
updates (clean and attacked FedAvg runs, seeds 1–3, all rounds):

| k | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 | 256 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mean error | 0.184 | 0.130 | 0.090 | 0.064 | 0.045 |
| 95th percentile | 0.445 | 0.317 | 0.222 | 0.161 | 0.113 |
| max | 0.644 | 0.479 | 0.343 | 0.246 | 0.171 |

- The error halves every time k quadruples (∝ 1/√k), as Johnson–Lindenstrauss predicts.
- Group structure survives at every k: mean Sybil–Sybil cosine 0.98, honest–honest 0.16,
  Sybil–honest −0.20 in both full and projected space. Because the Sybil/honest gap (≈ 0.8) is
  much larger than the distortion, detection stays near-perfect even at k = 16 (held-out F1
  0.991, FPR ≤ 0.6%; Figure 3). Smaller k needs a tighter threshold (tuned τ 0.12 at k = 16).
- At this attack strength there is no detection reason to use k > 64; k matters for margin
  against noise (E6 nuance) and for cost (E5).

## E3 — false positives and label skew (Figure 7)

No-attack false-positive rate at the τ tuned for α = 0.5 (seeds 1–2):

| α (label skew) | 0.1 (very non-IID) | 0.5 (default) | 5.0 (near-IID) |
|---|---|---|---|
| full-dim | 0.000 | 0.011 | **0.091** |
| projected k = 64 | 0.000 | 0.000 | **0.039** |

- **Counter to the usual worry**, false positives grow as data becomes *more IID*: honest clients
  with similar data produce similar updates, so they start to cluster (seen already on
  synthetic data, PROGRESS step 2). Strongly non-IID clients are dissimilar and never flagged.
- Under attack at α = 5 detection F1 drops to 0.90 (full) / 0.95 (projected) because of these
  false positives; recall stays high.
- Consequence for deployment: τ must be validated on data like the deployment's; a threshold
  tuned on skewed data over-flags near-IID populations.

## E5 — cost (Figure 6)

Synthetic Gaussian vectors, d = 46,730, k = 64, one CPU, median of 5 (timings vary run to run by
up to ~30%):

| clients n | 20 | 50 | 100 | 200 | 500 | 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| full-dim similarity (ms) | 3.6 | 10.4 | 23.7 | 52.8 | 235.4 | 632.0 |
| projected: matrix + projection + similarity (ms) | 21.4 | 22.3 | 28.7 | 40.4 | 75.2 | 127.3 |

- **Projection only pays off for larger n**: break-even lies between n = 100 and 200; at
  n = 1,000 it is 5× faster. Below that it is slower, because generating R_t costs a fixed
  ~15–18 ms per round. In the FL runs (n = 28) the projected detector took ~40–45 ms per round
  vs ~11–15 ms for the full one.
- Projection's arithmetic is O(n·d·k + k·d) against O(n²·d): the n² term is what projection
  removes; the k·d term (matrix generation) is what it adds.
- **Stored detection representations shrink 730×** (n·k vs n·d floats; 0.26 MB vs 187 MB at
  n = 1,000). The original updates must still be kept for aggregation, so total memory does not
  shrink by that factor.
- **Cryptographic randomness is essentially free**: keyed SHAKE-256 and PCG64 (same byte → bit
  layout) take the same time within ~12% at every k for d = 46,730 (15.2 vs 14.7 ms at k = 64);
  at d = 10,000 SHAKE took 1.6 ms vs 0.8 ms — twice as long but under 2 ms. Most of the cost is
  turning bits into a float matrix, not producing them.

## Hypotheses (plan §1) — verdicts

1. *Detection quality may approach the full-dimensional detector at suitable k* — **supported**:
   equal or better F1/FPR at every k ≥ 16 against this attack (thresholds tuned per variant).
2. *Projection reduces pairwise-similarity cost and stored representations* — **supported for
   storage (730×); for time only when n is large enough** (break-even n ≈ 100–200 here).
3. *Total runtime improves only in suitable regimes* — **confirmed**; slower below the break-even.
4. *Quality can deteriorate under strong non-IID data, high Sybil fractions or adaptive
   updates* — **partly**: deterioration came from near-IID data (not strong non-IID), and from
   adaptive updates when the attacker knows the projection; Sybil fraction was not swept.

## Limitations to state in the report

- One dataset and model, one attack (label flipping 7 → 1) with near-duplicate pool Sybils at a
  high fraction (29%). Unevaded detection is easy; E6 is the informative experiment.
- Seeds: tuning on 1, E2 on 3, E3/E6 on 2. E6's budget grid is coarse (the crossover for the
  key-secret attacker lies somewhere in 0.2–0.5).
- The E6 attacker uses random target directions, no detection feedback, and does not optimise
  against the clustering rule; a stronger adaptive attacker could do better (D10).
- Every run used a fresh generated server key, so runs are not bit-reproducible; they are
  compared statistically (set `SYBIL_FL_KEY` for exact reproduction).
- Runtimes come from one machine and vary between reruns.
- The server sees individual updates (no secure aggregation); identities are fixed; Sybil
  creation is not prevented, only its colluding updates detected.
- Cryptography: the HKDF → keyed SHAKE-256 composition is ours and unreviewed; KMAC256 would be
  the standardised keyed XOF. Security claims are about unpredictability of R_t under PRF
  assumptions, not a proof of detector security.
